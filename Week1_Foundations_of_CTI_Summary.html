01

What is Cyber Threat Intelligence?

#

1.1 Definition

CTI is data that is collected, processed, and analyzed to understand a threat actor's motives, targets, and attack behaviors. Its purpose is to help an organization stay ahead of threats and respond proactively rather than reactively.

The output of CTI is not a feed or a tool. It is a decision-ready product consumed by humans.

🔑
WORKING FORMULA
CTI = data + analysis + audience-tailored delivery.

1.2 Information vs. Intelligence

ConceptDescription
InformationRaw, unevaluated data such as a log line, an IP address, or a tweet, with no context or priority.
IntelligenceInformation that has been processed, analyzed, and contextualized for a specific decision-maker and use case.
⚠️
KEY WARNING
More data does not equal more intelligence. Analysis is what creates the value.

1.3 The Proactive-to-Reactive Spectrum

ModeExamples
ProactiveAnticipating attacks, hunting for adversaries already inside the network, prioritizing patches, briefing executives on emerging risk.
ReactiveIncident response: scoping a breach, attributing actors, and accelerating containment.

CTI supports both, but its highest return on investment comes from shifting the organization toward proactive defense.

The Six-Phase Intelligence Lifecycle

#

2.1 Overview

The lifecycle is a cycle, not a pipeline. Each phase feeds the next, and feedback returns to Direction, which closes the loop. It is repeatable and auditable rather than ad hoc. Phases 1 to 3 are Direction, Collection, Processing; phases 4 to 6 are Analysis, Dissemination, Feedback.

1Direction
2Collection
3Processing
4Analysis
5Dissemination
6Feedback
PhaseCore purposeKey activities / examplesOutput
1. Direction (planning)Set goals and requirements for the whole cycleIdentify at-risk assets, processes and personnel; estimate potential impact; decide actions and the types of intelligence neededPriority Intelligence Requirements (PIRs)
2. CollectionGather information that serves the PIRs (multi-source, continuous)Internal logs from SIEM, EDR, firewalls; commercial feeds (e.g., CrowdStrike Falcon X) and open feeds; OSINT; human sources; closed or dark-web forumsRaw, heterogeneous data
3. ProcessingConvert collected data into a usable, meaningful formatDecryption, translation, parsing and normalization (e.g., STIX/TAXII), filtering noise and known-benign entries, correlation across sourcesStructured, normalized, deduplicated dataset
4. AnalysisTurn processed data into intelligence that informs decisions (a human process)Decide whether to investigate further; block, contain, or monitor during an attack; improve existing measures; decide whether new controls are neededFinished intelligence tailored to each audience
5. DisseminationDeliver finished intelligence to the right audience, at the right time, by the right mediumSOC receives an IOC feed; executives receive a weekly brief; IR team receives ad hoc deep dives; choose medium and a follow-up contact pathRight intel, right audience, right time, right channel
6. FeedbackAssess whether goals were met and what should changeWere goals and PIRs met? Have priorities shifted? Did format and timing help consumers?Adjusted PIRs and refined process, fed back into Direction

2.2 Phase 1: Direction

Sets goals and requirements for the entire cycle. Without clear direction, downstream phases collect the wrong data and produce irrelevant intelligence.

  • Which assets, processes, and personnel are at risk and need protection
  • The potential impact if they were compromised
  • What actions should strengthen defenses
  • Which types of threat intelligence are required
💡
NOTE
This is the phase most often skipped in immature programs.

2.3 Phase 2: Collection

Gathers information useful for meeting the goals. It should be multi-source and continuous.

  • Internal logs and metadata from security tools
  • Threat data feeds, commercial and open
  • OSINT: news, blogs, vendor advisories
  • Human sources: peers and industry groups
  • Closed or dark-web forums

2.4 Phase 3: Processing

Converts collected data into a meaningful format. It can be done by machines, humans, or both.

  • Decryption of intercepted or encoded traffic
  • Language translation of foreign-language sources
  • Parsing and normalization into a common schema such as STIX/TAXII
  • Filtering out noise and known-benign entries
  • Correlating data across sources to enrich indicators

2.5 Phase 4: Analysis

Fundamentally a human process that turns processed information into intelligence that informs decisions.

  • Whether to investigate a potential threat further
  • Which action to take in an active attack: block, contain, or monitor
  • How to improve existing security measures
  • Whether additional controls are needed
💡
NOTE
Format matters: a SOC analyst needs an IOC list, an executive needs a one-page brief.

2.6 Phase 5: Dissemination

Delivers finished intelligence to the teams that benefit from it. Audience-tailoring is the core skill.

  • How can it be presented so the audience understands it?
  • How often should this audience receive updates?
  • Which medium should be used?
  • What is the contact path for follow-up questions?
💡
NOTE
Output: the right intelligence, for the right audience, at the right time, through the right channel.

2.7 Phase 6: Feedback

The final phase, and the one the deck warns is most often skipped. It closes the loop back into Direction.

  • Were the original goals and PIRs met?
  • Have priorities shifted (new assets, threats, executives)?
  • Did the dissemination format and timing help consumers?
  • How can the whole program improve?
💡
NOTE
Skipping Feedback turns the lifecycle into a one-shot pipeline.
03

The Four Types of CTI

#

3.1 Comparison

Each type is defined by its audience, time horizon, and the decision it supports. Choosing the wrong type for the audience wastes the intelligence.

TypeDefinitionAudienceTime horizonExample output
Operational (OTI)Real-time information about active threats and ongoing campaigns; supports situational awareness and incident responseIR leads, security managers, fraud detection teams, network defendersHours to daysThreat event briefs, active-campaign alerts, attacker capability assessments
Strategic (STI)Long-term planning and trend analysis; informs high-level security and risk postureCISO, executives, board risk committee, senior leadershipMonths to yearsWhite papers, executive briefings, risk posture reports, budget justifications
Tactical (TTI)Information about attacker Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs); supports threat hunting and defense planningIT managers, security operations managers, security admins, threat huntersWeeks to monthsForensic reports, TTP catalogs, threat hunting playbooks, mitigation guidance
TechnicalAtomic indicators of compromise (IOCs) such as IPs, domains, hashes, and email headers; enables rapid automated detection and blockingSOC analysts, IR teams (SIEM/EDR)Hours to days (short lifespan)IOC feeds, block lists, malicious IP/domain/hash lists, phishing email headers

3.2 Operational Threat Intelligence (OTI)

Real-time information most useful for responding to active threats. It draws on chat rooms, social media, antivirus logs, and historical occurrences to give context about security events and incidents.

  • Disclose potential risks early
  • Gain insight into offender methodologies
  • Establish patterns in past malicious activity
  • Investigate malicious activity more economically
  • Understand a threat actor's intention, capability, and opportunity to attack

Consumers: security managers, IR leads, network defenders, security forensics teams, and fraud detection teams.

3.3 Strategic Threat Intelligence (STI)

Focuses on long-term planning and trends. It describes the organization's overall security and risk posture and shapes strategies to mitigate risk. It is delivered as white papers, reports, and briefings, and informs budget, staffing, and strategic security investment. Consumers: the CISO, the board risk committee, and senior leadership.

3.4 Tactical Threat Intelligence (TTI)

Gathers information about a specific threat and identifies mitigations. It describes the TTPs attackers use and supports threat hunting. It comes from white papers, inter-organization sharing, and third parties, and is usually presented as forensic reports. Its key advantage is enabling proactive hunting before IOCs are observed. Consumers: IT managers, security operations managers, security administrators, and threat hunters.

3.5 Technical Threat Intelligence

Describes the resources an attacker uses, such as command-and-control channels, tools, and infrastructure. It has a shorter lifespan than tactical intelligence and focuses on specific IOCs, which enables rapid distribution and response. Examples: malicious IP addresses, domains, phishing email headers, and malware hash checksums. It is often paired with operational intelligence and consumed by SOC and IR teams through SIEM and EDR block lists.

🔑
RULE OF THUMB
The higher the audience sits in the organizational chart, the longer the time horizon and the less technical the intelligence should be. Mismatching audience and type is the most common CTI delivery failure. IOCs go stale fastest, while TTPs decay more slowly.
04

CTI Tools

#

4.1 Five Features of an Effective Tool

A CTI tool helps collect and analyze threat information from external sources, protecting the enterprise from existing vulnerabilities and preparing it for future ones.

FeatureWhat it means
1. Data-drivenCollects data from public, gated, and third-party sources to produce reliable, cross-validated information
2. Externally focusedIntegrates with internal systems for detection, but its primary scan target is external feeds and emerging-threat sources, which helps protect against zero-day attacks
3. FlexibleAdapts to diverse use cases: SOC enrichment, executive briefings, incident response support, and threat hunting
4. ComprehensiveScans volumes of external feeds covering threat information from around the world, not one region or vertical
5. ExtensibleIntegrates with SIEM, SOAR, EDR, ticketing, and identity systems

Pick a tool that scores well on all five, because missing any one creates blind spots.

05

Why CTI Matters

#

5.1 Five Benefits

#BenefitExplanation
1Reduces risk of cyber attacksEarly warning of threats targeting the organization's assets and sector lets defenders close gaps before adversaries exploit them.
2Increases staff efficiencyCTI prioritizes alerts, enriches indicators, and gives analysts context to triage faster.
3Prevents financial lossA single breach can cost millions in remediation, fines, lost business, and reputation; CTI reduces likelihood and severity.
4Supports security-informed decisionsExecutives need an understanding of the threat landscape to make defensible budget, staffing, and risk-transfer decisions.
5Keeps organizations ahead of adversariesCTI tracks shifts in TTPs and infrastructure so defenses evolve with the threat.
🔑
BOTTOM LINE
CTI converts the cost center of 'security' into a measurable risk-reduction function with quantifiable return on investment.

5.2 Mini-case 1: Regional Bank (Financial Services)

A mid-size bank's external threat surface platform flagged a new look-alike domain (a typosquat of its login page) and correlated it with credential lists on a dark-web forum. The CTI team sent a tactical brief to the SOC (TTI) and a strategic one-pager to the CISO (STI), and an operational alert (OTI) flagged the threat. The bank enforced MFA on the targeted customer segment within 24 hours and blocked the domain at the DNS layer via Cisco Umbrella.

The deck estimates about 18,000 account takeovers prevented, at a response cost under $25K against an estimated $4.2M avoided loss.

5.3 Mini-case 2: Hospital Network (Healthcare)

A hospital network's CTI feed reported a ransomware affiliate scanning healthcare VPN endpoints for a specific CVE. The operational alert reached incident response within minutes, the affiliate's TTPs went to threat hunters, and technical IOCs were pushed to EDR block lists across all 12 facilities. The vulnerable VPN appliance was patched network-wide within six hours. No encryption events occurred, and the deck estimates more than $8M avoided.

💡
PATTERN
In both cases CTI worked because each type (operational, tactical, technical) reached its correct audience within the correct time window.

5.4 Common Misconceptions

MythReality
'CTI is the same as a SIEM.'A SIEM correlates internal logs to detect anomalies; CTI focuses on external threat data and contextualizes internal events. They are complementary. A SIEM without CTI is deaf to the outside world, and CTI without a SIEM cannot enrich internal events.
'More data always means better intelligence.'Volume without processing and analysis produces noise. A well-tuned feed of 200 high-fidelity indicators routinely outperforms a firehose of 200,000 unfiltered ones.
'CTI is only useful after an attack.'Its highest return is proactive: threat hunting, patch prioritization, executive briefings, and risk-transfer decisions all happen before an attack.

Test yourself: when a vendor pitches CTI, ask which of these three myths they are implicitly relying on.

Applied Quantitative Scenarios

#

6.1 Seven Scenarios by Lifecycle Phase

Each scenario builds the quantitative reasoning the course expects. Values are reproduced as given in the deck.

#Phase / conceptProblemResult and interpretation
S1Direction: residual riskDatabase server with L = 0.6, I = 9, E = 0.8, control effectiveness Ce = 0.25. Compute R = L × I × E × (1 − Ce).R = 0.6 × 9 × 0.8 × 0.75 = <b>3.24</b>, treated as high. Raising Ce to at least 60% gives R = 1.73, so the asset is a top priority this quarter. The deck's claim that banks treat anything above 2.5 as top-tier is given without a source.
S2Processing and Analysis: layered detectionThree independent layers (email filter p = 0.7, EDR p = 0.6, network IDS p = 0.8). Probability at least one detects a phishing campaign.Miss = 0.3 × 0.4 × 0.2 = 0.024, so P(detect) = <b>0.976 (97.6%)</b>. A fourth layer with p = 0.5 would push detection above 98.8%.
S3Dissemination and Feedback: cost optimizationEach update costs $100; each hour of stale intelligence costs $25. Find the update interval h that minimizes cost.C(h) = 100/h + 25h/2, so h² = 8 and <b>h ≈ 2.83 hours</b>. If delay cost is 25h with no averaging factor, h = 2 hours. Both derivations are valid.
S4Analysis: majority votingThree analysts, each 80% accurate, vote independently.P = C(3,2)(0.8)²(0.2) + (0.8)³ = 0.384 + 0.512 = <b>0.896 (89.6%)</b>, a gain of 9.6 points. The gain disappears if all read the same flawed source.
S5Feedback: geometric decayMiss rate starts at 40% and falls 25% each quarterly cycle. Rate after four cycles?m₄ = 0.40 × (0.75)⁴ ≈ <b>12.66%</b>, a 68% reduction in a year. It never reaches zero: diminishing returns.
S6Direction: risk rankingA (L 0.6, I 9, E 0.7, Ce 0.30), B (L 0.3, I 10, E 0.9, Ce 0.10), C (L 0.8, I 6, E 0.5, Ce 0.45). Rank for patching.R(A) = 2.646, R(B) = 2.430, R(C) = 1.320. Ranking: <b>A, B, C</b>. No single factor determines priority.
S7Processing: feed fusion (OR policy)Feed 1 (TPR 0.70, FPR 0.03) and Feed 2 (TPR 0.60, FPR 0.02). Alert if either fires.Combined TPR = 1 − (0.30 × 0.40) = <b>0.88</b>; FPR = 1 − (0.97 × 0.98) ≈ <b>0.0494</b>. AND policy: FPR ≈ 0.0006 but TPR only 0.42. Use OR when missing a threat is costly (finance, healthcare), AND when analyst capacity is limited.
07

Key Takeaways & Terminology

#

7.1 Six Takeaways

These will be tested in tutorial and on the midterm.

  1. CTI = data + processing + analysis + context, produced for a specific decision-maker. Information is not intelligence.
  2. The lifecycle has six phases (Direction, Collection, Processing, Analysis, Dissemination, Feedback), closed by Feedback returning to Direction.
  3. There are four types of CTI (Operational, Strategic, Tactical, Technical), each matched to a different audience and time horizon.
  4. Effective CTI tools are data-driven, externally focused, flexible, comprehensive, and extensible.
  5. CTI reduces risk, increases staff efficiency, prevents financial loss, supports decisions, and keeps organizations ahead of adversaries.
  6. Quantitative reasoning (residual risk, detection probability, cost optimization, voting accuracy, feedback decay, feed fusion) is a core analyst skill.

Assessment, Tutorial & Reading

#

8.1 Assessment Overview

Five multiple-choice and five true/false questions with one-line justifications. Topics include:

  • PIRs as the output of Direction
  • Classifying an executive-facing trend report as Strategic
  • The five features of CTI tools
  • (1 − Ce) as the probability that existing controls fail
  • The effect of an AND-fusion policy on TPR and FPR
  • The Feedback phase is not optional
  • Majority voting among three 80%-accurate analysts yields 89.6%
  • A 25% quarterly reduction produces geometric decay, not a 100% reduction

8.2 Tutorial: Draft a Direction-Phase Brief

Individual task, about 90 minutes. Write a one-page Direction-phase brief (A4, 11pt) for a fictional company, Meridian Logistics: 1,200 employees, three regional warehouses, an AWS-hosted customer portal, and a legacy ERP. The CISO wants to know what the new CTI program should focus on next quarter.

Required sectionRequirementPoints
1. ASSETSList 4 to 6 named, concrete assets, including at least one data store, one network segment, one identity system, and one third-party dependency.2
2. STAKEHOLDERSThree groups (one executive, one technical, one external) and the type of CTI each needs.2
3. PIRsExactly three PIRs written as answerable questions, e.g. 'Which ransomware affiliates are currently targeting logistics ERP vendors?'2
4. SCOPE & TIMEFRAMEState the quarter, in-scope threat categories, and out-of-scope items. Keep it realistic and bounded.2
5. SUCCESS METRICOne quantitative metric, e.g. 'reduce miss rate from X to Y'.2
BonusCite at least one real-world CTI source (vendor blog, ISAC, or peer-reviewed paper).+1
