01

Introduction to CTI Frameworks

#

1.1 Lecture Roadmap

PartMain subjectWhat to remember
1Introduction to CTI frameworksA framework is a methodology, not a software product.
2Diamond ModelFour connected features used to decompose an intrusion.
3Cyber Kill ChainSeven sequential stages from reconnaissance to objectives.
4MITRE ATT&CKTactics describe WHY; techniques describe HOW.
5Pyramid of Pain, STIX, OSINT, CTIMIndicator value, sharing, collection, and intelligence classification.
6CTI platformsSoftware operationalises frameworks and can enrich SIEM events.
🔑
CENTRAL IDEA
The frameworks are complementary lenses. A single incident can be represented as a Diamond Model event, placed along the Kill Chain, and described in greater detail with ATT&CK.

1.2 Definition

A CTI framework is a structured methodology for collecting, analysing, and disseminating threat intelligence. It gives analysts a shared vocabulary and a repeatable way to describe adversary activity, turning scattered observations into comparable and correlatable records.

Why frameworks matter:

  • They encourage defenders to think like attackers and make adversary progression visible.
  • They expose gaps in detection coverage.
  • They help shorten the time between first observation and informed action.
  • Without a framework, intelligence can remain a flat list of indicators that is difficult to reason about strategically.

1.3 Framework vs. Platform

FrameworkPlatform
NatureA methodology or conceptual model: how analysts think and structure analysis.A software product: what analysts run to automate workflows.
ProvidesVocabulary, analytic steps, and classification schemes.Feed ingestion, IOC storage, correlation, sharing APIs, and dashboards.
ExamplesDiamond Model, Kill Chain, ATT&CK, Pyramid of Pain, STIX, OSINT methodology, CTIM.MISP, OpenCTI, Anomali, Recorded Future, ThreatConnect.
LimitDoes not ingest feeds or trigger detections on its own.Still needs frameworks and analysts to interpret its output.
⚠️
COMMON EXAM TRAP
A threat-feed aggregator, subscription service, API, or CTI platform is not itself a CTI framework.
02

Diamond Model of Intrusion Analysis

#

2.1 Four Vertices and Six Edges

The model represents an intrusion event through four edge-connected vertices. It asks who conducted the activity, what capability was used, what infrastructure was involved, and who or what was targeted.

VertexMeaningQuestions to ask
AdversaryThe actor or group behind the attack.Who is responsible? What are their motivation, objectives, TTPs, sophistication, and resources?
CapabilityThe tools, malware, exploits, or methods used.What did the adversary use to conduct the activity?
InfrastructureThe systems and resources used to deliver, control, or support the attack.Which domains, IP addresses, servers, or other infrastructure were involved?
VictimThe target of the intrusion.Who or what was targeted? Which organisation, person, system, or asset?

The four vertices are connected by six pairwise relationships. The connections matter because an analyst can pivot from one known feature to another. A known malicious domain may be linked to an infrastructure node, then to a capability, and then to an adversary or victim.

2.2 Meta-features

Meta-featureWhat it records
TimestampWhen the event or observation occurred.
PhaseWhere the event fits in the intrusion progression.
ResultThe outcome of the activity.
DirectionThe direction of the relationship or activity.
MethodologyHow the activity was carried out.
ResourcesResources used or involved in the event.

2.3 From Individual Events to Campaigns

LevelDescription
EventA single observed intrusion activity represented as a Diamond.
ThreadA related sequence or collection of events.
Activity GroupA broader grouping of related malicious activity.
CampaignA coordinated set of activity that can be linked through analysis.

Pivoting means following shared vertices or relationships across events to discover links. Repeated infrastructure, capabilities, or victim patterns can connect separate observations and support campaign attribution.

🔑
REMEMBER
The Diamond Model answers 'what are the core features and relationships of this intrusion?' It is not a chronological seven-step model.
03

Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain

#

3.1 Seven Stages

The Kill Chain describes an intrusion as a sequence of seven stages. It shows defenders where a control could interrupt the attack. The lecture presents it as a linear model: breaking a stage can prevent the attacker from reaching later objectives.

1Reconnaissance
2Weaponization
3Delivery
4Exploitation
5Installation
6Command and Control
7Actions on Objectives
StageWhat happensPossible defensive focus
1. ReconnaissanceThe attacker gathers information about the target.Reduce exposed information; monitor suspicious scanning and probing.
2. WeaponizationThe attacker prepares a payload or exploit for the target.Threat analysis, malware analysis, and controls that identify malicious payloads.
3. DeliveryThe payload is transmitted to the victim, for example by email or a link.Email/web filtering, attachment controls, user awareness.
4. ExploitationA vulnerability or user action triggers the malicious code.Patching, exploit prevention, endpoint protection.
5. InstallationMalware or another mechanism is installed on the target.Endpoint detection, application control, persistence monitoring.
6. Command and Control (C2)The compromised system communicates with attacker-controlled infrastructure.Network monitoring, DNS analysis, egress filtering, C2 detection.
7. Actions on ObjectivesThe attacker performs the intended activity, such as theft, disruption, or impact.Access control, data-loss prevention, monitoring, incident response.

3.2 How to Apply It

  1. Read the incident narrative in chronological order.
  2. Assign each event to the most appropriate Kill Chain stage.
  3. For each stage, identify a control that could detect or prevent progression.
  4. Look for the earliest practical interruption point, but do not assume every real attack follows a perfectly linear path.
⚠️
LIMITATION
The Kill Chain is a useful high-level, linear view, but it can simplify complex or non-linear activity. Use ATT&CK for more granular behaviour and the Diamond Model for relationships among intrusion features.
04

MITRE ATT&CK

#

4.1 Core Concepts

ATT&CK is a knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques, more granular than a simple high-level attack sequence.

ConceptMeaningMemory aid
TacticThe adversary's objective or goal at a point in the attack.WHY
TechniqueThe method used to achieve a tactic.HOW
Sub-techniqueA more specific variation or implementation of a technique.More detailed HOW
⚠️
COMMON MISCONCEPTION
Tactics are not the HOW and techniques are not the WHY. Correct: tactic = WHY, technique = HOW.

4.2 The 14 Enterprise Tactics

#TacticGeneral objective
1ReconnaissanceGather information to plan future operations.
2Resource DevelopmentEstablish resources to support operations.
3Initial AccessGain an initial foothold in the target environment.
4ExecutionRun malicious code or commands.
5PersistenceMaintain access across interruptions or restarts.
6Privilege EscalationObtain higher-level permissions.
7Defense EvasionAvoid or bypass defensive controls.
8Credential AccessObtain account credentials or authentication material.
9DiscoveryLearn about the compromised environment.
10Lateral MovementMove through the environment to other systems.
11CollectionGather data of interest.
12Command and ControlCommunicate with systems under attacker control.
13ExfiltrationSteal or transfer data out of the environment.
14ImpactManipulate, disrupt, or damage systems and data.

4.3 Kill Chain and Detection Coverage

The Kill Chain gives a broad sequence of stages. ATT&CK describes specific adversary objectives and techniques, so it adds detail to events already placed in the Kill Chain.

An organisation can map its detection rules to ATT&CK tactics and techniques to see where it has visibility and where gaps remain. A percentage of tactics covered is a simple breadth measure; it does not measure the depth or quality of detections.

05

Pyramid of Pain

#

5.1 Indicator Ranking

The Pyramid of Pain ranks indicators by how difficult or costly they are for an adversary to change after defenders detect them. The higher the indicator type, the more pain its detection causes.

Level (bottom to top)Indicator typeWhy it matters
1Hash valuesEasy to change by recompiling or modifying a file; lowest pain.
2IP addressesCan often be changed by moving infrastructure.
3Domain namesCan be replaced, though doing so may require operational changes.
4Network / host artifactsBehavioural traces or artifacts can require more effort to alter.
5ToolsReplacing or modifying tools can disrupt an operation.
6TTPsChanging tradecraft may require retraining operators and redesigning the operation; highest pain.
🔑
KEY INTERPRETATION
Hashes are precise identifiers, but precision is not the same as adversary pain. A hash changes quickly when a file is rebuilt. TTPs are harder to change because they reflect the way the adversary operates.
06

STIX, TAXII & OSINT

#

6.1 STIX vs. TAXII

STIX (Structured Threat Information eXpression) is a standardised, machine-readable language for representing and sharing CTI. It is a data representation standard, not a software product or collection tool.

TAXII (Trusted Automated Exchange of Intelligence Information) is the transport mechanism used to exchange CTI, including STIX-formatted information, between systems.

TermRoleSimple analogy
STIXDescribes and structures the threat information.The language / format
TAXIIMoves or exchanges the information between systems.The delivery / transport mechanism

6.2 Five STIX Building Blocks

ComponentPurpose
STIX Domain Objects (SDOs)Represent CTI concepts such as threat actors, campaigns, indicators, malware, and tools.
Cyber-observable Objects (SCOs)Represent observable technical facts, such as files, IP addresses, domains, and network activity.
STIX Relationship Objects (SROs)Describe relationships between STIX objects.
STIX Meta ObjectsProvide supporting metadata, markings, and other contextual information.
STIX BundlesPackage multiple STIX objects together for exchange.

6.3 OSINT Framework

OSINT means Open-Source Intelligence. The OSINT Framework is a structured collection methodology and directory of tools and sources for gathering information from publicly available sources. It supports collection and enrichment; it is not itself a CTI platform.

Tool named in the lectureTypical use
MaltegoVisualise relationships among people, domains, infrastructure, and other entities.
SpiderFootAutomate open-source reconnaissance and data collection.

CTIM Classification Matrix

#

7.1 Two Axes

AxisCategories / stages
Intelligence typeStrategic, Operational, Tactical, Technical.
Attack lifecycle stagePre-Attack; Initial Access; Execution & Persistence; Exfiltration & Impact.

7.2 Four Intelligence Types

TypeDescription
StrategicHigh-level implications, trends, and business or geopolitical context.
OperationalInformation about campaigns, adversary operations, and staging activity.
TacticalInformation about adversary methods and behaviours that defenders can use operationally.
TechnicalTechnical indicators and observables such as IP addresses, domains, and other IOCs.

The matrix stops analysts treating all intelligence as the same. A leadership briefing, a campaign assessment, a detection engineering task, and an IOC blocklist each need a different kind of intelligence.

08

CTI Platforms & SIEM Integration

#

8.1 What Platforms Do

A CTI platform is a software solution that lets an organisation collect, analyse, and share information about potential cyber threats. Platforms operationalise frameworks by turning structured intelligence into workflows, correlations, visualisations, and security monitoring inputs.

  • Ingest threat feeds and STIX-formatted indicators
  • Store and manage indicators of compromise (IOCs)
  • Correlate indicators, events, and relationships
  • Map detections to MITRE ATT&CK tactics and techniques
  • Visualise relationships that can be analysed using the Diamond Model
  • Share intelligence through APIs or feeds and enrich downstream security tools

8.2 Platform Examples

CategoryExamplesGeneral characteristics
Open-sourceMISP, OpenCTICan be self-hosted; community-supported; the organisation manages deployment and maintenance.
CommercialAnomali ThreatStream, Recorded Future, ThreatConnectVendor-supported products, generally subscription-based.
Intelligence / security feeds mentionedIBM X-Force Exchange, Palo Alto WildFireProvide intelligence or threat data that supports detection and enrichment.

8.3 SIEM Integration

A SIEM collects and analyses security events. A CTI platform exports curated indicators and mappings through STIX/TAXII feeds, and the SIEM ingests them to enrich raw events. If a user login involves a known malicious IP address, the SIEM can flag it using the CTI feed. A process mapped to an ATT&CK technique can raise an alert when observed.

SIEMRole in the workflow
SplunkIngests events and supports searches, correlations, dashboards, and alerts.
IBM QRadarCorrelates security events and supports threat monitoring.
Microsoft SentinelCloud-based SIEM/SOAR capability for collecting and analysing security data.
Elastic SecurityUses telemetry and analytics to detect and investigate threats.

Benefits of integration:

  • Adds context to raw events, helping analysts tell routine from suspicious activity
  • Improves prioritisation by associating events with known indicators, campaigns, and techniques
  • Supports repeatable detection and incident investigation workflows
  • Makes threat intelligence more actionable by connecting analysis to security controls

8.4 Best-Practice Reminders

  • Use reliable, relevant, and timely intelligence sources.
  • Validate and contextualise indicators before deploying them as blocking rules.
  • Track freshness and confidence; indicators can become stale or generate false positives.
