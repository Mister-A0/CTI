01

Introduction & the Three CTI Levels

#

1.1 Raw Information vs. Intelligence

Cyber Threat Intelligence is the process of collecting, processing, analyzing, and interpreting information about threats so an organization can make better security decisions. Raw information is an observation or data point. Intelligence is information that has been analyzed and placed into context so it can support a decision.

1.2 The Three Levels

Each level has a different audience, time horizon, type of information, and operational purpose.

LevelWhat it containsMain usersTypical time horizon
Level 1 – Threat IndicatorsAtomic indicators such as file hashes, IP addresses, domains, and URLs.Security tools, SOC analysts, automated controls.Minutes to days
Level 2 – Threat Data FeedsCorrelated indicators, statistics, reports, surveys, and malware-analysis outputs.SOC teams, incident responders, detection engineers.Days to weeks
Level 3 – Strategic CTISpecific adversaries, underground-economy activity, motivations, and TTPs.Security leadership and strategic decision-makers.Months to years
🔑
KEY RELATIONSHIP
As the level increases, information becomes more contextual and useful for broader decisions. It takes longer to produce, but it usually degrades more slowly than individual indicators.
02

Level 1: Threat Indicators

#

2.1 Indicators of Compromise (IOCs)

An IOC is an observable artifact that may indicate malicious activity or that a system has been compromised.

  • File hashes: fingerprints used to identify known files or malware samples.
  • IP addresses: addresses associated with suspicious infrastructure, such as command-and-control (C2) servers.
  • Domains: names linked to phishing, malware hosting, or other suspicious activity.
  • URLs: specific web addresses associated with malicious content or campaigns.

2.2 Hashes and Their Limitations

AlgorithmOutput sizeUse in the lecture
MD5128 bitsFile fingerprint / indicator.
SHA-1160 bitsFile fingerprint / indicator.

A hash is brittle: even a small change to a file can produce a different hash. A hash match is useful evidence, but a non-match does not prove a file is safe or unrelated.

2.3 Domain and IP Reputation

Reputation services assess whether a domain or IP has been associated with suspicious or harmful activity. The lecture lists nine high-risk categories:

  • Infrastructure hosting malware or spyware
  • Infrastructure distributing spam
  • Phishing pages designed to steal information
  • Peer-to-peer (P2P) services or anonymous proxies associated with suspicious activity
  • Command-and-control (C2) servers
  • Servers used for data exfiltration
  • Untraceable or suspicious darknet-related IP addresses
  • Newly registered domains, particularly those less than 30 days old
  • Legitimate websites that have been compromised and are being misused
⚠️
CAUTION
A single characteristic is not proof of maliciousness. A newly registered domain deserves closer inspection, but age alone does not establish that it is malicious.

2.4 Honeypots and Indicator Creation

A honeypot is a decoy system or service designed to attract and observe activity that may be malicious.

StagePurpose
1. Honeypot collectionCapture interactions, requests, payloads, and other activity directed at the decoy.
2. Static analysisInspect collected files or artifacts without executing them.
3. Dynamic analysisObserve behavior by running a sample in a controlled environment.
4. Signature / reputation outputCreate a detection signature or reputation assessment based on the analysis.

2.5 Static vs. Dynamic Analysis

Static analysisDynamic analysis
Examines a file or artifact without running it.Runs the sample in a controlled environment and observes behavior.
Can inspect file properties, strings, structure, and embedded indicators.Can reveal network connections, process activity, file changes, and other runtime behavior.
Does not directly show what the sample does during execution.Behavior may depend on the environment or execution conditions.

2.6 Industry Sources for Threat Feeds

  • Antivirus and domain-reputation vendors
  • Commercial Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) firms
  • Independent security laboratories and researchers
  • Open-source projects and Real-time Blackhole List (RBL) providers
  • Government and industry information-sharing groups
03

Level 2: Threat Data Feeds

#

Level 2 moves beyond isolated indicators. It combines and correlates information to find patterns, trends, and relationships that are more useful for operational decisions.

3.1 Correlation and Pattern Identification

A single IP address or hash may be ambiguous. When indicators are correlated with other observations, analysts can recognize a campaign, identify related infrastructure, or determine whether multiple events are connected.

3.2 Statistics, Reports, and Surveys

Information typeMain question it answersPurpose
StatisticsWhat is happening?Quantifies activity, frequency, distribution, or trends.
ReportsWhy is it happening?Explains incidents, campaigns, causes, and context.
SurveysWhat are peers doing?Shows practices, experiences, and approaches across organizations.
💡
MEMORY AID
Statistics = what; reports = why; surveys = what peers do.

3.3 Malware Sandbox Behavior as Feed Information

A sandbox is a controlled environment where suspicious software can be executed and observed. Typical behaviors in malware-analysis output:

  • Creating unusual registry entries
  • Attempting to disable antivirus or other security controls
  • Searching for files that may contain administrator information or passwords
  • Making outbound connections to command-and-control infrastructure
  • Creating connections that may stage or exfiltrate data
⚠️
SCOPE
Week 4 uses malware-analysis output as an input to Level 2 feeds. The full study of static and dynamic malware-analysis techniques comes in Week 6.
04

Level 3: Strategic CTI

#

Strategic CTI focuses on the adversaries that may target a particular organization, their motivations and capabilities, and the threats the organization may face soon. It supports planning and leadership decisions rather than only immediate blocking or alerting.

4.1 The Underground Economy

The ecosystem in which cybercriminals exchange tools, access, data, and services:

  • Forums and marketplaces: places where actors communicate, advertise, and trade.
  • Exploit kits and tools: malicious capabilities offered for sale or use.
  • Criminal services: fake-website creation, password cracking, rent-a-botnet services, DDoS-as-a-Service, and bulletproof hosting.
  • Stolen digital assets: credit-card data, PII, credentials, and session tokens.

4.2 Adversary Motivations

Adversary categoryTypical motivation or objective
CybercriminalsFinancial profit, including theft or sale of credentials, payment-card data, and PII.
Espionage actorsAcquisition of intellectual property, business plans, bids, or political and defense-related intelligence.
HacktivistsPromoting a cause, gaining publicity, or harassing targets; targets may be broad.

4.3 Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs)

TTPs describe how an adversary operates. They are generally more durable than atomic indicators, because an attacker can change an IP, domain, or hash while using similar methods. The lecture ties TTP analysis to MITRE ATT&CK and the Pyramid of Pain.

Five methods for deducing TTPs:

  1. Monitor underground forums and social media
  2. Track discussion of exploits and tools
  3. Observe purchases or offers in marketplaces
  4. Analyze malware behavior
  5. Monitor the sale or exchange of stolen assets
⚠️
TRIANGULATE
No single signal proves attribution or fully explains an adversary's methods. Use multiple sources and treat conclusions with appropriate confidence.
05

Domain Collection & Investigation

#

Domain information is useful at all three CTI levels: a domain can be an atomic indicator, part of a correlated feed, or evidence in a strategic campaign investigation.

5.1 WHOIS

WHOIS retrieves domain registration information, which may include the registrant or owner, registrar, creation date, and expiration date.

⚠️
IMPORTANT LIMITATION
WHOIS alone is not enough to attribute a domain to a person or group. Privacy-protection services, false details, and outdated records can make registration data incomplete or misleading.

5.2 Domain Investigation Tools

ToolMain purpose described in the lecture
urlscan.ioExamines website infrastructure, HTTP headers, and technologies used by a website.
LookylooVisualizes website redirect chains.
VirusTotalAggregates multi-engine reputation information about files and domains.
ShodanSearches for internet-connected devices and exposed services.
MaltegoVisualizes relationships among entities and OSINT findings.
ICANN LookupProvides domain registration lookup information.
WHOIS queryRetrieves available registration records for a domain.

5.3 Five Domain-Collection Best Practices

  1. Use multiple sources: combine different tools and data sources rather than relying on one result.
  2. Verify information: corroborate important findings before acting on them.
  3. Stay up to date: ownership, hosting, and reputation can change.
  4. Focus on relevance: collect what supports the investigation instead of collecting everything.
  5. Protect privacy: handle personal information responsibly and consider requirements such as GDPR and CCPA.
06

Misconceptions & Mini-Cases

#

6.1 Common Misconceptions

MisconceptionCorrect understanding
All indicators are equally reliable.Indicators differ in quality, context, and confidence. A single indicator should not automatically be treated as proof.
More feeds automatically mean better coverage.More feeds can create duplication and noise. Relevance, quality, and correlation matter.
WHOIS alone can identify the attacker.Registration records may be private, false, or outdated and do not prove attribution.
Malware analysis is the same as strategic CTI.Malware-analysis findings can feed Level 2; strategic CTI focuses on adversaries, motivations, and TTPs.

6.2 Case 1: SOC Investigation of a Phishing Look-alike Domain

A SOC team identifies a domain that resembles a legitimate organization. Analysts correlate its registration age, reputation information, and passive DNS observations. If the evidence supports a malicious assessment, the team blocks the domain through a DNS firewall and quarantines emails containing matching links.

💡
LESSON
Combine multiple sources and connect intelligence to an operational response.

6.3 Case 2: Strategic CTI for a Healthcare Organization

Underground discussions suggest that credentials associated with healthcare organizations are being brokered, a phishing kit is being promoted, and ransomware affiliates are active. Together, these signals may point to an emerging campaign. The organization prepares by reviewing MFA coverage, EDR readiness, and incident-response procedures.

💡
LESSON
Strategic CTI uses multiple signals to anticipate risk and guide preparation.

Quantitative Scenarios

#

7.1 Bayesian Inference: Probability a Hit Is Malicious

Given: base rate = 5%, true-positive rate (TPR) = 0.90, false-positive rate (FPR) = 0.02.

StepCalculation
Bayes' theoremP(Malicious | Hit) = P(Hit | Malicious) × P(Malicious) / P(Hit)
Numerator0.90 × 0.05 = 0.045
P(Hit)(0.90 × 0.05) + (0.02 × 0.95) = 0.045 + 0.019 = 0.064
Posterior0.045 / 0.064 = 0.703125 ≈ 70.3%

With these base-rate and test characteristics, about 70.3% of hits are expected to be malicious.

7.2 Detection Timing and Noise Filtering

ScenarioGivenResult
Detection timing300 events/hour; 15% are true threats300 × 0.15 = 45 threats/hour; 60 / 45 = 1.33 minutes per true threat on average
Noise filtering50 GB/day; filter removes 96% as noise50 × (1 − 0.96) = 50 × 0.04 = 2 GB/day remaining

7.3 Threat-Feed Bandwidth

Given 12,000 IOCs, 3 KB per IOC, and an update every 10 minutes:

StepCalculation
Data per update12,000 × 3 KB = 36,000 KB ≈ 36 MB
Updates per day24 × 60 / 10 = 144
Daily volume36 MB × 144 = 5,184 MB; with 1 GB = 1,024 MB, ≈ 5.06 GB/day per feed

This assumes the stated per-IOC size and excludes transport or protocol overhead.

7.4 Precision, Recall, and F1-Score

Given TP = 360, FP = 90, FN = 140, TN = 9,410.

MetricCalculationResult
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP) = 360 / 45080%
RecallTP / (TP + FN) = 360 / 50072%
F12 × (0.80 × 0.72) / (0.80 + 0.72)≈ 75.8%

Precision measures how many alerts classified as positive are actually positive. Recall measures how many actual positives are detected. F1 combines the two.

7.5 Domain-Age Risk Scoring

Given 1,200 domains, 15% newly registered, and 40 risk points per young domain: young domains = 1,200 × 0.15 = 180; total = 180 × 40 = 7,200 risk points per month.

8.2 Quick Knowledge Check: Multiple Choice

1. Which hash algorithm mentioned in the lecture produces a 128-bit output?

A. SHA-1 B. MD5 C. SHA-256 D. AES

Answer: B. MD5

2. Which tool is used to visualize relationships among OSINT entities?

A. Shodan B. Lookyloo C. Maltego D. ICANN Lookup

Answer: C. Maltego

3. Which CTI level focuses most on adversaries, motivations, and TTPs?

A. Level 1 B. Level 2 C. Level 3 D. None

Answer: C. Level 3

4. The Bayesian example gives what posterior probability?

A. 5% B. 45% C. 70.3% D. 90%

Answer: C. 70.3%

5. Which is a domain-collection best practice?

A. Trust one source B. Collect everything C. Verify findings using multiple sources D. Ignore privacy

Answer: C. Verify findings using multiple sources

6. Which tool visualizes website redirect chains?

A. Lookyloo B. Shodan C. Maltego D. WHOIS

Answer: A. Lookyloo

7. Which motivation is most associated with cybercriminals?

A. Financial profit B. Publicity only C. Political intelligence only D. Academic research

Answer: A. Financial profit

8. Why is WHOIS alone insufficient for attribution?

A. It never returns dates B. Records may be private, false, or outdated C. It only works for IP addresses D. It executes malware

Answer: B. Records may be private, false, or outdated

9. Which is an example of Level 2 information?

A. One isolated hash B. A correlated threat report C. A board-level adversary assessment only D. A password

Answer: B. A correlated threat report

10. What does C2 stand for?

A. Certificate-to-certificate B. Command and control C. Control-to-cloud D. Computer-to-computer

Answer: B. Command and control

Answer: False. WHOIS may be incomplete, protected, false, or outdated.

Answer: True.

Answer: False. Full malware-analysis techniques are covered in Week 6; Week 4 uses analysis output.

Honeypots can simulate services or systems to attract and observe activity.

Answer: True.

Answer: True.

8.4 Practical Lab: Investigating Suspicious Domains

Investigate three suspicious domains using WHOIS or ICANN Lookup and VirusTotal. For each domain, record:

  • Domain name
  • Registrar
  • Domain age or creation date
  • VirusTotal score or detection summary
  • Your verdict (for example, suspicious, likely malicious, or inconclusive)
  • A short justification supported by the collected evidence

Reflection: write about 200 words on what you learned, which sources were useful, and why multiple sources matter.
